\begin{abstract}
  Proportional representation is typically evaluated by asking whether parties
  receive seats in proportion to votes. In fragmented legislatures, however, the
  politically relevant unit is often not a party but a coalition. This article
  examines coalition-level election inversions in Brazil's Chamber of Deputies.
  A coalition inversion occurs when a set of parties controls a legislative seat
  majority while receiving less than half of the national federal-deputy vote.
  Using party vote totals, elected-seat totals, cabinet-period coalition data,
  and party ideology classifications for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 federal-deputy
  elections, I identify inversions in two analytically distinct objects:
  observed cabinet coalitions and ideological intervals defined over the
  election-year party set. Five observed cabinet periods satisfy the inversion
  criterion: two during the mandate that followed the 2014 election, two during
  the mandate that followed the 2018 election, and one during the mandate that
  followed the 2022 election. The two 2018 cases each combine 47.25 percent of
  the vote with exactly 257 seats. An all-interval ideological search finds
  minimal connected interval inversions in 2014 and 2022, but none in 2018,
  including a compact 2022 interval with 45.35 percent of the vote and 258 of
  513 seats. An accounting decomposition of the five observed inversions shows
  how their national seat differentials divide into within-district allocation
  and between-district weighting components. The results show that Brazil's
  federally allocated and highly fragmented proportional system can generate
  coalitional inversions: party sets with less than half of the national vote
  can nevertheless command legislative majorities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electoral systems convert votes into seats, and those seats define the legislative conditions under which governments are formed. Proportional representation is usually defended on the grounds that this conversion preserves the distribution of electoral support more faithfully than plurality or majoritarian systems. That claim, however, is often evaluated at the party level. In fragmented party systems, political authority is frequently organized at the coalition level. Governments are formed by party sets, and those party sets may obtain legislative majorities without receiving national vote majorities.

This article examines when party-level deviations from proportionality aggregate
into seat majorities without vote majorities among observed cabinet coalitions
and ideologically constrained potential coalitions in Brazil’s Chamber of
Deputies. Its analytical focus is the aggregation  through which
representational differentials distributed across parties become politically
consequential by crossing the legislative majority threshold. 

Most evaluations of proportionality stop before the point at which parliamentary
authority is exercised. A party with a positive seat differential has more seats
than its national vote share would imply, while another with a negative
differential has fewer. These are familiar objects in the study of apportionment
and proportional representation. But coalition formation aggregates these
differentials. A set of parties may accumulate enough positive differentials to
cross the seat-majority threshold before crossing the vote-majority threshold.
When this happens, the legislature contains a majority that is not backed by a
corresponding national vote majority for the same set of parties.

The existence of coalition inversions is already established.
Kurrild-Klitgaard
\parencite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres} identifies
such inversions in Danish government formation, while Miller
\parencite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres} shows that they remain
possible even under highly proportional national allocation and documents
numerous inversion possibilities in empirical election results. The present
analysis differs in both its political objects and its institutional setting.
Rather than treating governing coalitions as election-level alternatives, I
reconstruct the parties actually represented in cabinet and follow changes in
cabinet composition over the legislative term. I then complement these realized
party sets with a systematically defined domain of potential coalitions
constrained by ideological proximity. Finally, these coalitions are evaluated in
Brazil's multidistrict system, where the national Chamber aggregates
twenty-seven independent district allocations without a national compensatory
tier. The question is therefore not whether coalition inversions can occur, but
when they appear across realized and ideologically constrained coalition
configurations under this distinct form of proportional representation.


Brazil provides a substantively different setting from the cases that motivate
the existing inversion literature. Federal-deputy seats are allocated
independently in twenty-seven electoral districts and then aggregated into the
national Chamber, without a compensatory national tier. The relevant allocation
rules also changed across the elections considered, including proportional
electoral coalitions in 2014 and 2018 and party federations in 2022. Coalition
majorities are therefore assembled nationally from party seat totals produced
through a heterogeneous multidistrict proportional system rather than through a
single national allocation.

The contribution is threefold. First, I reconstruct the party sets actually
represented in cabinet and follow changes in cabinet composition over the
legislative term, providing a longitudinal analysis of realized cabinet
configurations rather than treating government formation as a single
election-level coalition comparison. Second, I complement these realized
configurations with a systematically defined domain of potential coalitions
constrained by ideological proximity. Third, I examine both coalition domains
in a federal multidistrict PR system whose national legislature is generated by
the aggregation of independent district allocations. Across these objects, the
analysis asks when party-level representational differentials are sufficient to
change coalition majority status.


The empirical comparison produces a domain-specific contrast. Cabinet
configurations satisfying the inversion criterion appear in the mandates
generated by all three elections: two after 2014, two after 2018, and one after
2022. The ideologically constrained potential-coalition analysis finds eight
inversions in 2014 and six in 2022, but none in 2018. The two domains therefore
diverge in 2018: PSC-inclusive cabinet coalitions reach exactly 257 seats
without a vote majority, while every seat-majority ideological interval also
has a vote majority. This comparison shows that coalition definition and
configuration determine whether fixed party-level differentials become
majority-changing.

\section{Coalition inversions under Brazilian proportional representation}
\label{sec:theory}
The literature on proportional representation identifies several ways in which seat allocations can depart from democratic and proportional intuitions. These departures include voting paradoxes in list PR, threshold effects, apportionment effects, spoiler effects, manipulation, unequal representation, and allocation problems internal to party lists or coalitions \parencite{nurmi1999voting,Saari_2001,van1993paradoxes,balinski2010fair,kaminski2002parties,kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system,flis2019pot_ladle,wada24_unified_approac_to_measur_unequal_repres}. The present analysis isolates one object within that broader family: coalition-level election inversions. The point is not that PR generally fails, but that proportionality evaluated at the party level does not settle how seats behave once parties are recombined into politically relevant sets.

Kurrild-Klitgaard's earlier analysis of voting paradoxes under proportional
representation provides part of the broader background
\parencite{kurrild2008voting}, while his later study of Danish government
formation is the direct empirical predecessor for coalition inversions
\parencite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres}. That
analysis identifies inversion cases among politically meaningful governing
alternatives and relates them to threshold and federal effects. Miller
\parencite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres} generalizes the result,
showing that coalitionwise inversions remain possible even under pure
proportional representation and using party seat differentials to identify
inversion possibilities in empirical election configurations. Existing work
therefore already contains both realized governing examples and searches over
potential party sets. The distinction pursued here lies elsewhere: realized
coalitions are operationalized through the parties actually represented in
cabinet over successive cabinet periods, while potential coalitions are drawn
from an explicitly specified ideological domain. Both are then evaluated in a
multidistrict system structurally different from the national or nationally
compensated PR environments emphasized in the predecessor literature.

This article therefore treats coalition inversions as one PR pathology among others, not as the whole problem of proportional representation. The argument parallels recent work on electoral-system pathologies in mixed systems. Institutions designed to combine proportional and majoritarian virtues can also generate paradoxes, strategic vulnerabilities, overhang-seat problems, compensation failures, and contamination effects \parencite{FlisGrofmanKaminski2025Introduction,moon25_parad_mixed_semi_compen_system_south_korea,tanacs-mandak25_hackin_mixed_elect_system,Behnke2025SpectacularEnlargement,flis25_cancel_overh_seats,vucicevic25_legac_past}. The present paper makes an analogous move for Brazilian PR. It asks how a system usually defended by party-level proportionality can still generate majority-status distortions once parties are aggregated into cabinets, ideological blocs, or other politically consequential coalitional sets.


The conceptual point requires care in the Brazilian case because the word
``coalition'' can refer to different institutional objects. In the abstract PR
literature, a coalition is usually a set of parties grouped after seats have been
allocated. It is not itself the list to which votes are initially assigned. Brazil,
however, has also used collective electoral units. In 2014 and 2018, parties
could compete for federal-deputy seats through proportional electoral coalitions
(\emph{coligações proporcionais}). In 2022, after the end of proportional
electoral coalitions, party federations (\emph{federações}) operated as collective
electoral units. These electoral units are distinct from the coalitions analyzed
in this article.

The inversion test is applied to post-electoral party sets, not to electoral
lists as such. Observed cabinet coalitions are sets of parties that participate
in government during a cabinet period. Ideological intervals are analytically
constructed sets of parties that are contiguous in an ideological ordering. Both
are reconstructed after the election from party-level vote and seat totals.
Thus, even when some seats were originally won through \emph{coligações} or \emph{federações},
the empirical question is whether a later party set, translated into the
relevant election-year party labels, crosses the Chamber seat-majority threshold
without crossing the national vote-majority threshold.

With this distinction in place, the coalitionwise logic becomes straightforward.
A proportional formula may be monotonic with respect to the electoral units to
which it allocates seats: if list $A$ receives more votes than list $B$, the rule
will not ordinarily assign fewer seats to $A$ than to $B$, setting aside
thresholds, district effects, and other institutional complications. But this
does not imply monotonicity for every post-electoral set of parties. A cabinet
coalition or ideological interval may combine parties whose seats were produced
through different district contests and, in some years, through different
electoral coalitions or federations. A party set can therefore cross the
seat-majority threshold even if the same set falls short of a national vote
majority.

Spoiler effects provide a second bridge between coalition inversions and the
broader literature on PR pathologies. In PR systems, spoilers need not operate
only by changing which party wins a plurality. They can operate by removing
votes from the seat-allocation stage. When parties fail to cross legal or
effective thresholds, their votes do not translate into seats, and the seat
shares of the remaining parties are inflated. \textcite{kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system} analysis of Polish
parliamentary elections shows that these effects can be large under D'Hondt PR.
In the 2015 election, for example, PiS won 37.58 percent of the vote and 51.09
percent of the seats, a seat majority produced in part by votes lost by parties
outside the seat distribution. The Brazilian cases analyzed here are different
because the object is not a single-party manufactured majority but a
post-electoral party set. The mechanism is nevertheless related. Spoilers and
wasted votes change the distribution of effective seats across parties, while
coalition formation recombines those party-level differentials after the
election.

For the analysis that follows, the relevant distinction is between
representational advantage and majority-changing representational advantage. A
party set may receive more seats than its national vote share would imply and
yet remain a seat minority. An inversion occurs only when the surplus associated
with a particular coalition configuration is large enough to carry that set
across the legislative majority threshold. This threshold-crossing perspective
provides a common way to compare realized cabinets, potential coalitions, and
near-miss cases without treating every instance of overrepresentation as an
inversion.
\section{Brazil's multidistrict proportional system}


The Brazilian case differs from the benchmarks used in the direct inversion
literature in a way that matters for the empirical object studied here. The
Chamber is not generated by a single national seat allocation, nor are
district-level deviations subsequently corrected by a national compensatory
tier. Instead, the national distribution of party seats is the aggregate outcome
of twenty-seven separate electoral districts. Coalition vote and seat totals are
therefore national aggregates constructed only after the district-level
allocation process has been completed.

Brazilian proportional representation is not a national list system. Federal
deputies are elected in the states and the Federal District, each of which forms
a separate multimember district. The Chamber has 513 seats, distributed across
these districts according to population, subject to a constitutional floor of eight
seats and a ceiling of seventy seats per unit
\parencite{camaraDeputadosNumeroEstados}. As a result, the national Chamber is
not produced by applying a single proportional formula to national party vote
totals. It is produced by aggregating twenty-seven district-level proportional
allocations.

Within each district, Brazil uses open-list proportional representation. Voters may
cast nominal votes for candidates or party-label votes, but these votes are
aggregated at the relevant collective unit for seat allocation. Depending on the
election year, this unit may be a party, an electoral coalition, or a federation.
The electoral quotient defines the number of votes corresponding to a district
seat; the party quotient determines how many seats the relevant list initially
receives; and remaining seats are allocated by averages
\parencite{tse2023quocientes,tse2022federacoes}. Elected candidates are then
selected from within the list, subject to individual-vote requirements. The ballot
is candidate-centered, but seat allocation is first mediated by collective partisan
units.

This electoral structure connects directly to coalitional presidentialism. Since no
presidential party normally controls a seat majority alone, presidents must govern
by assembling party coalitions. Abranches's formulation of
\emph{presidencialismo de coalizão} identifies the combination of presidentialism,
multipartism, federalism, proportional representation, and coalition management as
a defining feature of Brazilian democracy \parencite{abranches1988presidencialismo}.
Figueiredo and Limongi revise the pessimistic interpretation of this arrangement:
Brazilian presidents do not simply bargain with atomized deputies, but form
governments through parties, distribute ministries, and rely on party leaders in a
centralized legislative organization \parencite{figueiredo2000presidential}.
Pereira and Mueller add a complementary account of executive-legislative exchange
through budgetary and distributive resources \parencite{pereira2004cost}.

The implication for this article is that the politically relevant vote-seat
conversion is not only the electoral unit's individual conversion, but also the
post-electoral party set's aggregate conversion. A governing coalition is assembled
from parties whose seats were produced in separate district contests and sometimes
through prior electoral groupings. Once those parties are grouped nationally, their
seat total may cross the Chamber majority threshold even if their combined national
federal-deputy vote does not. The analysis does not ask whether such coalitions
are stable, programmatic, or legislatively cohesive. Nor does it infer voter
preferences over cabinets from party votes. It asks whether a party set, once translated into the party labels used in the
corresponding election returns, receives enough seats to command a Chamber
majority while receiving less than half of the national vote in that election.

\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:mea}

Following \textcite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres}, let $\Parties$ be the set of parties in a federal-deputy election. For each party
$i \in \Parties$, let $v_i$ denote the party's national federal-deputy vote total,
let $s_i$ denote the number of Chamber seats won by that party, and let $V =
\sum_{i\in\Parties} v_i$ be the total national vote. Since the Chamber has 513
seats, the proportional seat quota of party $i$ is
\(
  \Quota_i = 513\frac{v_i}{V}. \)
The party's seat differential is \(  \Diff_i = s_i - \Quota_i.\)

Positive values mean that the party received more seats than its national vote
share would imply under exact proportionality. Negative values mean that the
party received fewer. Since quotas sum to 513 and seats sum to 513, party-level
seat differentials sum to zero.

To characterize how these differentials vary with party size, I also use the
party-level representation ratio \parencite{taagepera1980proportionality}
\[
R_i
=
\frac{s_i/513}{v_i/V}.
\]
A value of $R_i=1$ denotes exact proportionality, values above one indicate
overrepresentation, and values below one indicate underrepresentation; parties
that receive votes but win no seats have $R_i=0$. The representation ratio and
the seat differential describe the same party-level deviation in different
units, since
\[
d_i=q_i(R_i-1).
\]
The representation profile therefore provides a normalized view of the
party-level deviations that are subsequently aggregated within coalitions.


For any coalition $C \subseteq \Parties$, define
\[
  v_C = \sum_{i\in C} v_i, \qquad s_C = \sum_{i\in C} s_i,
\]
\[
  q_C = 513\frac{v_C}{V}, \qquad d_C = s_C - q_C.
\]
The coalition analogue of the party representation ratio is
\[
R_C
=
\frac{s_C/513}{v_C/V}
=
\frac{s_C}{q_C}
=
1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}.
\]
It describes the coalition's relative representation compared with exact
proportionality. Because \(q_C=\sum_{i\in C}q_i\), the same quantity is the
quota-weighted average of its members' party ratios:
\[
R_C
=
\sum_{i\in C}\frac{q_i}{q_C}R_i.
\]
This identity links the party representation profile to the coalition-level
accounting.

A coalition has a vote majority when $v_C/V > .5$ and a seat majority when
$s_C \geq 257$. A coalition inversion occurs when a party set $C$ has a seat
majority but not a vote majority:
\[
s_C \geq 257
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\frac{v_C}{V}< .5.
\]
Equivalently, $C$ is a Chamber majority without being a national
federal-deputy vote majority.

For a coalition without a vote majority, define the additional absolute seat
differential required to reach a Chamber majority as
\[
r_C = 257-q_C.
\]
Since \(s_C=q_C+d_C\), the inversion condition can equivalently be written as
\[
\frac{v_C}{V}< .5
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
d_C\geq r_C.
\]
This formulation separates relative coalition representation from the
absolute surplus needed to change majority status. In particular, \(R_C>1\)
(equivalently, \(d_C>0\) when \(q_C>0\)) means that a coalition is
overrepresented, but it is not sufficient for inversion. Its accumulated
differential must be at least as large as the differential required to reach
257 seats.

The five observed inversions can also be decomposed across electoral districts.
For district \(d\), let \(v_{Cd}\) and \(V_d\) be the coalition and total valid
votes, and let \(s_{Cd}\) and \(S_d\) be the coalition and total seats. Then
\[
d_C=A_C+B_C,
\]
where
\[
A_C=\sum_d\left(s_{Cd}-S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}\right)
\]
is the within-district allocation component and
\[
B_C=\sum_d S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}-513\frac{v_C}{V}
\]
is the between-district weighting component. This is an accounting identity,
not a causal decomposition. In particular, \(B_C\) is not ``pure
malapportionment.'' It combines the district weights embodied in national
aggregation, including seat apportionment, turnout and valid-vote differences,
and coalition vote geography.

\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

The empirical analysis combines TSE federal-deputy vote totals, elected-seat
totals, cabinet-composition data, and party ideology classifications. Vote and
seat data for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 elections come from the Tribunal Superior
Eleitoral (TSE), accessed through the \texttt{electionsBR} package
\parencite{electionsbr}. Cabinet composition is reconstructed from the
Brazilian Wikipedia cabinet-member lists for the Dilma Rousseff, Michel Temer,
Jair Bolsonaro, and Lula III administrations. These sources identify ministerial
appointments by office, incumbent, party label, entry date, and exit date. I use
these appointment records to construct cabinet periods, defined by changes in
the set of parties holding ministries or ministry-level offices\footnote{Further
  implementation details, including party-label harmonization and translation
  tables, are documented in the replication repository: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/electoral-inversion-B6B5/README.md}.}.

Cabinet periods are linked to elections by mandate-window overlap. The 2014
election is linked to cabinet periods active from January 1, 2015 through
December 31, 2018; the 2018 election to periods active from January 1, 2019
through December 31, 2022; and the 2022 election to the observed Lula III window
beginning on January 1, 2023 and continuing through March 19, 2026, the latest reliable cabinet-period endpoint in the source data. Coalition vote and seat totals are computed using the party labels and
party-level totals from the election that produced the corresponding Chamber
delegation. These inputs are used to construct two analytical objects:
observed cabinet-period coalitions and ideological intervals defined over the
election-year party set.
Each party set is evaluated by its national vote share, seat share, quota, seat
differential, and coalition-inversion status.

The national vote measure is the valid federal-deputy vote credited to each
party under its election-year label. This includes both nominal votes and
party-label votes, including valid nominal votes later treated as party-label
votes in the TSE aggregation.\footnote{For 2014, the vote measure is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS + QT\_VOTOS\_LEGENDA}. For 2018 and 2022, it is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS\_VALIDOS + QT\_TOTAL\_VOTOS\_LEG\_VALIDOS}.}

The ideology analysis uses external party classifications to order parties on a
left-right scale. The 2014 and 2018 orders use \textcite{bolognesi2023nova}. The 2022 order
uses the later 2018--2022 update, operationalized through the 2025 data release
and associated article \parencite{bolognesi2025dataverse,bolognesi2026desaparecimento}.
This creates a methodological caveat: the 2014 ordering retrojects later expert
classifications onto an earlier party system. Appendix \ref{app:ideology-order}
therefore reports the full party order, classification label, numeric ideology
value, source year, and source-party label used for each election.

allows the analysis to ask whether changes in realized cabinet composition move
the corresponding party set across the vote- and seat-majority thresholds even
though the underlying election result remains fixed.

in the short 2016.2 window, and PSD leaves in 2016.3. The 2016.4 period then
marks the post-impeachment reconfiguration: DEM, PP, PPS, PSB, PSD, PSDB, and PV
enter, while PCdoB, PDT, PR, and PT leave. PMDB and PTB remain across that
rupture. The 2017.1 coalition drops PTB and becomes the most important
Temer-period inversion. Later, DEM, PSB, and PV leave in 2018.1, and PTN enters
in 2018.2.

The 2018 mandate contains thirteen cabinet periods. The initial
Bolsonaro-period coalition contains DEM, MDB, NOVO, PATRIOTA, PR, PRB, and
PSL. PSDB briefly enters in 2020.1; MDB leaves in 2020.2; and PSD enters in
2020.3. PSC enters on December 9, 2020. PSL leaves the next day and returns on
March 29, 2021; NOVO leaves in 2021.2; and PP enters in 2021.3. The 2021.3 and
2022.1 coalitions each have 47.25 percent of the vote and exactly 257 seats, so
both are inversions. The final 2022 sequence is handled through the União Brasil
lineage rule: after the DEM--PSL fusion, UNIÃO is used to define cabinet-period
changes and is then expanded back to DEM and PSL for 2018 vote-seat accounting.
PSC leaves on March 30, 2022, ending the inversion and creating the two-day
2022.2 period at 250 seats; PSDB and PR then leave on successive transitions.

The 2022 mandate begins with MDB, PCdoB, PDT, PSB, PSD, PSOL, PT, REDE, and
UNIÃO. That initial 2023.1 coalition is an inversion. In 2023.2, PP and
Republicanos enter, converting the coalition into one with both a vote majority
and a seat majority. In 2025.1, UNIÃO leaves the cabinet party set in the
available data window; the coalition remains a vote-and-seat majority.

Because vote and seat totals are fixed by the election linked to each mandate,
variation within a mandate comes from changes in the set of parties represented
in cabinet. The cabinet-period comparison therefore isolates how successive
realized recombinations of the same election-generated party outcomes alter
coalition majority status.

Figure \ref{fig:timeline} shows why the inversion criterion is more demanding than ordinary
overrepresentation. The first two panels trace the vote and seat shares used to
classify each cabinet period, while the third plots the coalition representation
ratio. Of the 24 cabinet-period party sets, 21 have $R_C>1$; the exceptions are
the 2020.2, 2020.4, and 2021.1 Bolsonaro-period configurations. Thus, cabinet coalitions can be
systematically overrepresented as party sets without producing inversions. The
2014-linked mandate moves through the short 2016.2 impeachment-window inversion
and the more durable 2017.1 Temer inversion; the 2018-linked sequence crosses
the seat-majority threshold in 2021.3 and remains there through 2022.1; and the
initial 2022-linked Lula coalition inverts before later cabinet expansion carries
it above both majority thresholds.

The 2016.2 case should not be dismissed because of its short duration. It is
not evidence of a stable governing arrangement, but it is precisely for that
reason an important event-window result. The period falls inside the cabinet
realignment sequence surrounding the impeachment process: after PRB and PP
leave the cabinet party set, but before the subsequent loss of PSD reduces the
coalition below the seat-majority threshold. In that narrow window, the
remaining cabinet parties no longer correspond to a national federal-deputy
vote majority, with only 46.54 percent of the vote, but they still hold 259
seats. This is the largest observed cabinet inversion in the analysis by seat
differential. The result therefore identifies the point in the impeachment
sequence at which the representational asymmetry becomes most visible: a
cabinet party set associated with less than half of the national federal-deputy
vote still retained a Chamber majority.

The 2017.1 case is different. It is not the largest inversion by seat
differential, but it is the more durable Temer-period inversion. The
coalition composed of DEM, PMDB, PP, PPS, PSB, PSD, PSDB, and PV had 49.91
percent of the national federal-deputy vote and 266 Chamber seats. It therefore
crossed the 257-seat threshold while remaining just short of the vote-majority
threshold.

Figure~\ref{fig:vote-seat} cautions against interpreting the 2014 inversions
as the mechanical accumulation of a general large-party advantage. In that
election, positive and negative representation ratios occur among parties of
quite different sizes, so the coalition surplus is composition-specific rather
than the expression of a uniform party-size bias.

The 2022 result shows that the phenomenon is not confined to the impeachment
period. The initial Lula coalition, from January 1 to September 12, 2023, held
263 seats with 48.89 percent of the national vote. This coalition was therefore
also a seat majority without a vote majority. After PP and Republicanos enter
the coalition, the status changes: the 2023.2 coalition has 63.79 percent of the
vote and 350 seats. It remains overrepresented, but it is no longer an inversion
because it has both votes and seats.

The five observed inversions also differ in how they cross the threshold.
The 2017.1 coalition is threshold-adjacent: \(R_C=1.04\) and
\(d_C=9.95\), while only \(r_C=0.95\) is required. The 2023.1 coalition
has \(R_C=1.05\), \(d_C=12.20\), and \(r_C=6.20\). By contrast, the
2016.2 coalition begins farther below a vote majority and needs a much larger
absolute surplus: its \(R_C=1.08\) corresponds to \(d_C=20.27\), which
exceeds \(r_C=18.27\). The two PSC-inclusive 2018 periods share an election-space
party composition and therefore identical metrics: \(R_C=1.06\),
\(q_C=242.38\), and \(d_C=r_C=14.62\). Their surplus reaches, but does not
exceed, the amount required for exactly 257 seats.

\section{Minimum connected winning potential coalitions}
\label{sec:ideology}
The second coalition domain complements observed cabinet composition with a
systematic set of potential party combinations. Rather than searching all
subsets of parties, I restrict the baseline domain using Axelrod's connectedness
criterion: eligible coalitions must form contiguous sets in the election-year
ideological ordering. This does not imply that actual Brazilian cabinets are
normally connected, nor is connectedness treated as a theory of coalition
formation. It provides a transparent restriction on the otherwise combinatorial
space of possible party sets. I then relax the restriction by allowing at most
one omitted party inside the coalition's ideological span, testing whether the
results depend on exact no-gap connectedness.  

An ideological interval $[p_i,p_j]$ is a minimal connected interval inversion
when it is a coalition inversion and removing either endpoint destroys the seat
majority. This is minimality under contiguous endpoint deletion. It is precisely what \textcite{axelrod1970conflict} calls minimal connected winning coalitions. This definition is intentionally stricter than a generic search over all party
subsets and less restrictive than a search over observed cabinets only. It rules
out cherry picked non-contiguous collections, but it does not require that the
party set ever formed a cabinet. It shows whether there are seat-vote inversions
embedded in the ideological arrangement of parties.

The two coalition domains therefore answer different questions. Cabinet
composition identifies party sets that were institutionally realized, while the
ideological domain identifies structured coalition possibilities that need not
have entered government. Comparing them shows whether inversion is confined to
particular realized cabinet configurations or also appears in nearby regions of
the party system's ideological coalition space.

Figure \ref{fig:heatmaps} gives the interval analysis its substantive content.
Each heatmap shows all contiguous ideological intervals in the Chamber
delegation. A cell is defined by the interval's left endpoint and right endpoint

The distinction between orange and red cells is important. An inversion is any
contiguous interval with at least 257 seats and no vote majority. A minimal
inversion is an inversion that ceases to be seat-winning if either endpoint is
removed. Minimality 
identifies the critical connected bloc at which a vote-minority interval first
becomes a Chamber majority. Larger orange regions may be genuine inversions,
but many of them are expansions of a smaller red interval. The red cells
therefore mark the frontier of coalitional seat efficiency inside the ideological
order. Appendix \ref{app:minimal-connected-winning} reports the minimal
connected winning intervals, and the red cells are the subset of those intervals
that also satisfy the inversion criterion.

The main pattern is that the ideological location and compactness of inversions
change. In 2014, the inversion intervals are broad and cross the center. One
minimal connected coalitional inversion begins with PSB, a center-left party in
the ideology classification, and runs through PPS and PV before accumulating a
sequence of center-right and right parties. The other minimal connected
coalitional inversions begin farther to the right, but they still belong to an
extended center-to-right region rather than to a compact bloc at the right edge
of the party system. The 2014 pattern therefore suggests a diffuse form of
coalitional seat efficiency: inversions are produced by broad intervals that
gather enough parties across the middle and right of the ideological order.

The 2022 panel is different. The seat-majority intervals are more compressed,
and the inversion cells are displaced sharply to the right. The broad
MDB--UNIÃO interval begins in the center-right and ends in the far right. More
importantly, the PP--PL interval is entirely located on the right side of the
ideological order. It begins with PP, classified as right, and ends with PL,
classified as far right, passing through DC, REPUBLICANOS, PSC, UNIÃO,
PATRIOTA, and NOVO. This interval is not only compact. It is ideologically
one-sided. A bloc located at the right and far-right edge of the Chamber can
reach 258 seats with only 45.35 percent of the national federal-deputy vote.
The result is stronger than the mere presence of orange cells in the heatmap:
the minimal source of the inversion is already a narrow right-side bloc.

The aggregation identity also connects the PP--PL result to the party
representation profile in Figure~\ref{fig:vote-seat}. Because \(R_C\) is the
quota-weighted average of member-party \(R_i\) values, coalition
overrepresentation does not require every member to be overrepresented. What
matters for inversion is that the members' accumulated \(d_i\) values produce
a coalition differential \(d_C\) large enough to meet \(r_C\).

The figure also clarifies the weakness of the left in the Chamber. Left and
center-left parties do not form an autonomous seat-majority region in any of
the three panels. Intervals that begin on the left become seat majorities only
after they expand far enough into the center, center-right, or right. In this
sense, the left appears as the endpoint of broad coalitions, not as the core of a
compact seat-majority bloc. By contrast, in 2022 the right does form such a
core: the PP--PL interval shows that a relatively narrow right/far-right bloc can
cross the majority threshold without a corresponding vote majority.

The Appendix \ref{app:cabinet-interval-bridge} table reinforces this reading
and adds an important political qualification. PT held the presidency during
part of the mandate associated with the 2014 election, through Dilma
Rousseff's second administration, and again in the mandate associated with the
2022 election, through Lula's third administration. For that reason, PT is not
just another party in the interval order. It is the presidential party in two of the
three election-linked periods covered by the analysis. Yet PT appears in only
four of the twenty minimal connected winning intervals: two in 2014, one in
2018, and one in 2022. All four are vote-and-seat majorities, not inversions.
They are also broad intervals. The two PT-containing intervals in 2014 contain
12 parties, the 2018 interval contains 15 parties, and the 2022 interval contains
19 parties. This is a relevant contrast: minimal connected majorities that
include the presidential party require wide ideological spans and vote majorities,
while the vote-minority seat-majority intervals are located farther to the right.
The compact 2022 PP--PL interval is therefore not just another minimal winning
interval. It identifies a right-side bloc whose seat conversion is strong enough
to produce a Chamber majority without a national vote majority.

The 2018 panel isolates a useful null result within the ideological-interval domain.
It contains many seat-majority intervals, but none of them are inversions.
Contiguous ideological blocks could
become legislative majorities, but only after also crossing the vote-majority
threshold. This indicates that the absence of 2018 interval inversions is not due to the
absence of large contiguous ideological intervals. It is due to the fact that the
vote-seat conversion did not create a contiguous ideological bloc that was both
seat-majoritarian and vote-minoritarian. This interval result coexists with two
observed PSC-inclusive cabinet inversions in the same election-linked mandate;
the contrast follows from the distinct coalition domains.

Taken together, the three panels show a change in the ideological structure of
coalitional overrepresentation. In 2014, inversion possibilities are distributed
across broad center-to-right intervals. In 2018, seat-majority intervals remain
above the vote-majority threshold. In 2022, the inversion intervals move to the
right edge of the party system and become more compact. The Chamber's
right flank becomes sufficiently seat-efficient that a compact right/far-right
interval can command a legislative majority without a national vote majority,
while minimal connected majorities that include the left require broader
vote-majority coalitions.


The connection between the cabinet results and the interval results is developed
in Appendix \ref{app:cabinet-interval-bridge}. The key point is that observed
cabinets are not usually contiguous ideological blocs. They are sparse selections
from wider ideological spans. The Dilma coalitions are left-anchored but
punctured: they include PT, PCdoB, and PDT while extending into PMDB, PSD,
PR, PP, and PTB. The post-impeachment Temer coalitions remain punctured but
shift rightward, replacing the left anchor with a center-to-right configuration.
The 2023 Lula coalition returns to a left-anchored but very broad structure,
while the compact connected inversion in 2022 is not the Lula cabinet but the
right-side PP--PL interval. Thus, the cabinet analysis identifies realized
governing coalitions, while the interval analysis shows where seat-efficient
connected blocs are located in the ideological order.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

The contribution of the analysis is to compare the appearance of coalition inversions
across two politically defined coalition domains in a proportional system
institutionally different from the principal cases in the existing literature.
The first domain follows the parties actually represented in cabinet as cabinet
composition changes during a legislative term. The second constructs a
systematic set of potential coalitions restricted by ideological proximity. The
same vote-seat distribution can therefore be evaluated under realized and
structured potential recombinations of parties.

This distinction is especially relevant in Brazil because Chamber majorities
are assembled nationally from party seats generated in twenty-seven separate
district allocations. Party-level deviations from proportionality are fixed by
the election, but their coalitional consequence depends on which parties are
grouped together. Some configurations convert those deviations into a
vote-minority seat majority, while others remain below the legislative threshold.
The object of interest is therefore not disproportionality alone, but the
interaction between the electoral distribution of seats and the party sets
through which legislative and governing majorities are organized.

The 2014-linked mandate illustrates this possibility most sharply. The short
2016.2 period falls inside the impeachment realignment and is not evidence of a
stable cabinet configuration. Its importance lies elsewhere. During that
realignment, the remaining cabinet party set no longer corresponded to a
national federal-deputy vote majority, but it still held a Chamber majority.
The 2017.1 Temer coalition is the more durable case: 266 seats with 49.91
percent of the national federal-deputy vote. This case gives a precise
institutional form to the representational problem: the governing arrangement
was supported by a congressional majority whose seat status exceeded its
national vote basis.

The 2018-linked mandate reveals why the two coalition domains must be kept
separate. PSC-inclusive Bolsonaro cabinets in 2021.3 and 2022.1 each reach
exactly 257 seats with 47.25 percent of the vote. These observed coalitions
therefore invert for 238 covered days. Yet none of the 630 contiguous
ideological intervals in the 2018 party order is an inversion. The result is not
a common negative control: it is a within-election contrast between realized,
sparse cabinet coalitions and contiguous ideological blocs. Coalition inversion
is configuration-dependent even when the underlying party votes and seats are
fixed.

The 2022-linked mandate complicates the political interpretation in the opposite
direction. Lula's initial cabinet is also an observed inversion. It begins as a
seat majority without a national federal-deputy vote majority, then becomes a
vote-and-seat majority after PP and Republicanos enter. But the ideological
interval analysis shows a different structure beneath the cabinet sequence. The
most striking connected inversion is not the Lula coalition. It is the PP--PL
interval, a compact right and far-right bloc that controls 258 seats with only
45.35 percent of the national vote.

This contrast is central. The observed cabinet inversions show that
vote-minority seat-majorities can appear in actual government formation, while
the ideological interval inversions show where seat-efficient connected blocs
are located in the party system. The 2018 divergence shows that one domain can
contain inversions even when the other does not. In 2014, the inversion
intervals are broad and center-to-right. In 2022, they become more compact and
move to the right edge of the Chamber. The left appears as an endpoint of broad
coalitions. The right, by contrast, can form a narrower connected bloc with
majority seat status. Thus,
Brazil's open-list PR institutional matrix can allocate seats in such a way that
coalitional recombination creates Chamber majorities not backed by national vote
majorities.


This is why the Brazilian case belongs in a broader discussion of pathologies inside proportional representation. The analysis does not show that PR is generally inferior to majoritarian systems, nor that Brazilian representation is simply disproportional. It shows a more specific institutional pathology. A system may be evaluated as proportional at the party level while still producing majority-status distortions at the level where legislative authority is exercised. In that sense, coalition inversions are parallel to spoilers, threshold effects, apportionment effects, mixed-system paradoxes, and manipulation problems: each identifies a point at which the electoral rule translates votes into politically consequential seats in a way that is not visible from simple party vote shares alone \parencite{van1993paradoxes,kurrild2008voting,kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system,FlisGrofmanKaminski2025Introduction}.


The representation profiles also show that coalition inversion should not be
reduced to a single stable party-size bias. In 2014, overrepresentation and
underrepresentation are distributed relatively heterogeneously across parties
of different sizes; by 2022, positive representation ratios are much more
concentrated among larger parties. Coalition inversion is therefore an
aggregation phenomenon whose party-level composition can differ across
elections. The PP--PL case illustrates the point particularly clearly: large
positive differentials among several of its larger members more than compensate
for the negative differentials of its smaller members.

The accounting decomposition sharpens this multidistrict description without
assigning causal responsibility. The within-district component is positive in
all five inversions and is larger than the total differential in four, where a
negative between-district component partly offsets it. Only 2016.2 combines
positive within- and between-district components. Because the between-district
term jointly reflects seat apportionment, turnout and valid-vote differences,
and coalition vote geography, it cannot be interpreted as a pure measure of
malapportionment. Separating the causal contributions of these mechanisms would
require a counterfactual design beyond the accounting identity used here.

The party contribution vectors are similarly descriptive. In 2014 and 2018,
seats were often allocated through joint electoral lists, so party differentials
within the inversion coalitions are ex post accounting contributions rather
than independent party-level allocation mechanisms. The same noncausal caution
applies to all years: a large positive or negative \(d_i\) does not establish
that a party is responsible for producing the inversion.

The ideological analysis should also be treated as a structured diagnostic rather
than a final map of Brazilian party ideology. The classifications make it possible
to enumerate connected intervals, but the 2014 and 2018 orders depend on later
classification sources and party-label translation. Alternative placements may
shift endpoint labels, interval widths, or marginal cases.

The last limitation concerns interpretation. The vote share of a cabinet or
interval is not a direct vote for that cabinet or interval. It is the sum of votes
cast for parties later grouped into that set. The criterion therefore measures
coalitional representation, not voter endorsement. It also does not model
legislative discipline, roll-call support, or portfolio bargaining. These are
behavioral questions. The present object is prior to them: whether the electoral
translation gives a party set majority status in the Chamber.


\section{Conclusion}


This article extends the small literature on coalition inversions under
proportional representation in three directions. First, it brings cabinet
composition directly into the analysis, tracing how changes in the parties
actually represented in government can alter the vote--seat majority status of
a coalition over the course of a legislative term. Second, it complements these
realized configurations with a systematic analysis of ideologically constrained
potential coalitions, connecting the inversion problem to the structure of the
party system. Third, it examines these phenomena in a multidistrict
proportional system in which the national legislature aggregates twenty-seven
independent district allocations without national compensation. The Brazilian
case therefore extends the agenda opened by Kurrild-Klitgaard and Miller:
party-level deviations from proportionality, generated by the ordinary
operation of an electoral system, can accumulate across politically meaningful
party sets and become consequential at the level of legislative majority
formation.



The answer is not uniform across elections or coalition domains. After the 2014
election, cabinet inversions appear in the impeachment realignment and again in
the Temer period. After the 2018 election, PSC-inclusive cabinets generate two
inversions at exactly 257 seats, even though the ideological-interval search
remains null. After the 2022 election, Lula's initial cabinet begins as a seat
majority without a vote majority, but later becomes a vote-and-seat majority
after cabinet expansion. Connected ideological vote-minority seat-majorities
exist in 2014 and 2022, and the strongest 2022 case is the compact PP--PL
right-side interval.

Taken together, the results show that the political consequence of
party-level representational differentials depends on the party sets through
which they are aggregated. Changes in cabinet composition can move a realized
party set into or out of inversion status without any change in the underlying
election result, while the ideological analysis shows that inversion can also
appear in structured potential coalitions outside the cabinet domain. The 2018
contrast makes the distinction especially clear: the realized PSC-inclusive
coalitions invert, while the contiguous ideological intervals do not.

The decomposition takes the next accounting step for all five observed
inversions. It partitions each national differential into within-district
allocation and between-district weighting terms and verifies that the party and
district contributions sum to the coalition total. This establishes where the
observed differential resides in the accounting, not why it arose. Explaining
the causal roles of apportionment, district magnitude, turnout, party geography,
threshold rules, electoral coalitions, and federations remains a separate
counterfactual research agenda.

\section{Cabinet coalitions and ideological intervals}
\label{app:cabinet-interval-bridge}

Table \ref{tab:cabinet-interval-bridge} compares observed cabinet coalitions
with the ideological intervals that contain or approximate them. The bridge
table keeps two operations separate: cabinet periods are defined from the party
labels observed in the cabinet data, while ideological closures and vote-seat
calculations use the translated election-year party set. The interval analysis
therefore remains tied to the Chamber delegation produced by each election.
The connected closure of a cabinet is the smallest no-gap ideological interval
containing all cabinet parties. Gaps are parties inside that closure that are
not members of the cabinet coalition. The final columns report the closest
minimal connected winning interval and, where one exists, the closest minimal
connected inversion. This table links the observed coalition analysis to the
interval analysis by showing whether realized cabinet coalitions are compact
ideological blocs or sparse selections from a wider ideological span.

\input{table_appendix_cabinet_interval_bridge.tex}

The first result is that no observed cabinet coalition is itself a contiguous
ideological interval. Every cabinet period has gaps inside its connected closure.
The smallest number of gaps is eight, and several cabinet periods have more than
fifteen. This means that Brazilian cabinet coalitions are not simply connected
ideological blocs translated into government. They are selective coalitions: party
sets assembled from a wider ideological span while omitting many parties that
lie between their leftmost and rightmost members.

This matters for the interpretation of cabinet inversions. The observed cabinet
inversions are sparse coalitions, but their connected closures are not inversions.
All connected closures in Table \ref{tab:cabinet-interval-bridge} are
vote-and-seat majorities. The 2016.2 cabinet, for example, has 46.54 percent of
the vote and 259 seats, but its connected closure runs from PCdoB to PR and
contains 20 parties. That closure has 77.61 percent of the vote and 408 seats.
The inversion therefore does not come from a compact left-to-right ideological
bloc. It comes from a selective cabinet coalition that retains a seat majority
while omitting thirteen parties inside its ideological span.

The same logic applies to the more durable Temer-period inversion. The 2017.1
cabinet contains DEM, PMDB, PP, PPS, PSB, PSD, PSDB, and PV. Its connected
closure runs from PSB to DEM, contains 25 parties, and has seventeen gaps. The
observed cabinet has 49.91 percent of the vote and 266 seats, while the full
PSB--DEM closure has 78.42 percent of the vote and 409 seats. The cabinet
inversion is therefore not a connected ideological majority. It is a sparse
center-to-right cabinet coalition located near the connected inversion region
identified in the interval analysis. Its closest minimal connected winning
interval is PSB--PTN, which is itself an inversion with 48.92 percent of the vote
and 260 seats.

The Dilma coalitions have a different ideological profile from the later
Temer coalitions. They are also sparse, but they are left-anchored. The cabinet
sets before Temer include PT, PCdoB, and PDT while extending to parties such
as PMDB, PSD, PR, PP, and PTB. Their closures run from PCdoB to either PR or
PP, and their gap structure contains many center, center-right, and right parties
that are not cabinet members. The impeachment transition changes this structure.
From 2016.3 to 2016.4, PCdoB, PDT, PT, and PR leave, while PSB, PPS, PV, PSD,
PSDB, PP, and DEM enter. In the ideology classification, the parties leaving are
concentrated on the left and center-left, with one right party, while the parties
entering are concentrated in the center, right, and far right. The cabinet remains
punctured, but its ideological anchor shifts rightward.

The ideology diagnostics in the table confirm this transition. On the project's
ideology scale, the average unweighted cabinet ideology rises from the
pre-Temer sequence to the post-impeachment Temer sequence. The transition from
2016.3 to 2016.4 alone increases the unweighted cabinet ideology mean by 1.53
points and the seat-weighted mean by 1.49 points. This is the substantive
difference between the two sparse coalition types. The pre-Temer coalitions are
left-anchored and broad. The Temer coalitions are center-to-right and broad.
Both are non-contiguous, but they occupy different regions of the ideological
order.

The Bolsonaro-period cabinets provide a domain contrast. They are more
ideologically concentrated on the right and far right, and their connected
closures are narrower than the 2014 and 2022 closures. The PSC-inclusive
2021.3 and 2022.1 cabinets each have 47.25 percent of the vote and exactly 257
seats, so both observed coalitions are inversions. Their connected closure,
PSD--DEM, contains seventeen parties and eight gaps, has 57.37 percent of the
vote and 292 seats, and is therefore a vote-and-seat majority. The closest
minimal connected winning interval is PSDB--DEM, which has 51.52 percent of the
vote and 257 seats. Thus, the 2018 mandate contains observed cabinet inversions
but no ideological-interval inversion: the sparse realized party set and its
connected closure have different majority classifications.


The 2023.1 Lula cabinet returns to a left-anchored structure, but it is not a
compact left coalition. It contains PSOL, PCdoB, PT, PDT, PSB, REDE, MDB,
PSD, and UNIÃO. Its connected closure runs from PSOL to UNIÃO, contains 26
parties, and has seventeen gaps. The observed cabinet is an inversion, with
48.89 percent of the vote and 263 seats, but its connected closure has 80.64
percent of the vote and 407 seats. The closest minimal connected winning
interval is PT--PP, a broad vote-and-seat majority with 57.86 percent of the
vote and 284 seats. The closest minimal connected inversion is MDB--UNIÃO,
which lies to the right of the cabinet's left anchor.

This comparison is crucial for interpreting the 2022 result. The Lula cabinet is
a realized inversion, but it is not the compact connected inversion revealed by
the interval search. The compact connected inversion is PP--PL: an eight-party
right/far-right interval with 45.35 percent of the vote and 258 seats. The
observed Lula coalition reaches a seat majority through a broad and sparse
cross-ideological cabinet arrangement. The connected interval analysis shows
that the most compact vote-minority seat-majority bloc in the same Chamber is
located on the right.

The two analyses therefore meet at a specific structural point. Cabinet
coalitions identify realized governing party sets. Connected intervals identify
the seat-vote geometry of ideological blocs. The cabinet-to-interval comparison
shows that realized cabinets may draw on seat asymmetries without being
connected blocs themselves. In 2014, the pre-Temer coalitions are sparse and
left-anchored, while the Temer coalitions are sparse and shifted rightward. In
2018, two right-side cabinets reach exactly 257 seats without a vote majority,
while their connected closures have both vote and seat majorities. In 2022, the
Lula cabinet is a broad realized inversion, while the compact connected
inversion lies on the right. The gap between cabinet coalitions and connected
intervals is therefore not a nuisance. It shows how Brazil's coalition
presidentialism combines sparse cabinet construction with an ideological
distribution in which seat-efficient connected blocs are often located elsewhere.
