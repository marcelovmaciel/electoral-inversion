\begin{abstract}
  Proportional representation is typically evaluated by asking whether parties
  receive seats in proportion to votes. In fragmented legislatures, however, the
  politically relevant unit is often not a party but a coalition. This article
  examines coalition-level election inversions in Brazil's Chamber of Deputies.
  A coalition inversion occurs when a set of parties controls a legislative seat
  majority while receiving less than half of the national federal-deputy vote.
  Using party vote totals, elected-seat totals, cabinet-period coalition data,
  and party ideology classifications for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 federal-deputy
  elections, I identify inversions in two analytically distinct objects:
  observed cabinet coalitions and ideologically constrained potential
  coalitions defined over the election-year party set. Five observed cabinet
  periods satisfy the inversion
  criterion: two during the mandate that followed the 2014 election, two during
  the mandate that followed the 2018 election, and one during the mandate that
  followed the 2022 election. The two 2018 cases each combine 47.25 percent of
  the vote with exactly 257 seats. An exact-connected (\(k=0\)) ideological
  search finds minimal connected interval inversions in 2014 and 2022, but none
  in 2018, including a compact 2022 interval with 45.35 percent of the vote and
  258 of 513 seats. A nested one-gap (\(k=1\)) check preserves the 2014 and 2022
  inversion regions and reveals an inversion in 2018. Coalition representation
  ratios show that 21 of the 24 observed
  cabinet-period party sets are overrepresented, although only five also
  satisfy the inversion criterion. For the observed-cabinet and exact-connected
  baseline cases, the accounting identity \(d_C=A_C+B_C\) partitions each
  coalition differential
  into within-district allocation and between-district weighting components.
  Their signs and magnitudes differ across cases: the components sometimes
  reinforce one another and sometimes offset. The results show that Brazil's federally
  allocated and highly fragmented proportional system can generate coalitional
  inversions: party sets with less than half of the national vote can
  nevertheless command legislative majorities.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Electoral systems convert votes into seats, and those seats define the legislative conditions under which governments are formed. Proportional representation is usually defended on the grounds that this conversion preserves the distribution of electoral support more faithfully than plurality or majoritarian systems. That claim, however, is often evaluated at the party level. In fragmented party systems, political authority is frequently organized at the coalition level. Governments are formed by party sets, and those party sets may obtain legislative majorities without receiving national vote majorities.

This article examines when party-level deviations from proportionality aggregate
into seat majorities without vote majorities among observed cabinet coalitions
and ideologically constrained potential coalitions in Brazil’s Chamber of
Deputies. Its analytical focus is how party differentials combine into
coalition differentials, when the resulting representational advantage becomes
large enough to change majority status, and how each coalition differential
divides into within-district allocation and between-district weighting
components. The coalition representation ratio \(R_C\) measures this advantage
relative to the coalition's proportional quota, while \(d_C\) expresses the
same departure in seats.

Most evaluations of proportionality stop before the point at which parliamentary
authority is exercised. A party with a positive seat differential has more seats
than its national vote share would imply, while another with a negative
differential has fewer. These are familiar objects in the study of apportionment
and proportional representation. But coalition formation aggregates these
differentials. A set of parties may accumulate enough positive differentials to
cross the seat-majority threshold before crossing the vote-majority threshold.
When this happens, the legislature contains a majority that is not backed by a
corresponding national vote majority for the same set of parties.

The existence of coalition inversions is already established.
Kurrild-Klitgaard
\parencite{kurrild-klitgaard13_elect_inver_coalit_propor_repres} identifies
such inversions in Danish government formation, while Miller
\parencite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres} shows that they remain
possible even under highly proportional national allocation and documents
numerous inversion possibilities in empirical election results. The present
analysis differs in both its political objects and its institutional setting.
Rather than treating governing coalitions as election-level alternatives, I
reconstruct the parties actually represented in cabinet and follow changes in
cabinet composition over the legislative term. I then complement these realized
party sets with a systematically defined domain of potential coalitions
constrained by ideological proximity. Finally, these coalitions are evaluated in
Brazil's multidistrict system, where the national Chamber aggregates
twenty-seven independent district allocations without a national compensatory
tier. The question is therefore not whether coalition inversions can occur, but
when they appear across realized and ideologically constrained coalition
configurations under this distinct form of proportional representation.


Brazil is analytically useful not because it approximates the pure-PR
benchmark developed by \textcite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres},
but because it places the coalition-aggregation problem inside an electoral
system with several institutionally distinct sources of vote--seat deviation.
The Chamber aggregates twenty-seven independent district allocations, with
large differences in district magnitude, no national compensatory tier,
population-based seat apportionment subject to state floors and ceilings,
year-specific eligibility rules, and changing collective allocation units.
Proportional electoral coalitions pooled votes in 2014 and 2018, while party
federations performed a related but more durable role in 2022.

This institutional complexity does not invalidate the coalition accounting.
Whatever their origin, party differentials still aggregate as
\(d_C=\sum_{i\in C}d_i\), and a vote-minority coalition changes majority
status precisely when its accumulated differential closes the remaining
distance between its proportional quota and 257 seats. Complexity therefore
weakens any claim that a single mechanism caused a particular inversion, but
strengthens the substantive reason to study how heterogeneous
representational gains and losses accumulate, offset one another, and become
majority-changing \parencite{bochsler2026winners}.

The contribution is threefold. First, I reconstruct the party sets actually
represented in cabinet and follow changes in cabinet composition over the
legislative term, providing a longitudinal analysis of realized cabinet
configurations rather than treating government formation as a single
election-level coalition comparison. Second, I complement these realized
configurations with a systematically defined domain of potential coalitions
constrained by ideological proximity.

Third, I examine both coalition domains in a state-districted proportional
system containing multiple sources of national disproportionality. The
analysis does not attempt to identify a single institutional cause for each
case. It instead asks how the realized party-level differentials generated by
that system aggregate across politically relevant party sets and cross the
discontinuous 257-seat majority threshold. For inversion cases, the identity
\(d_C=A_C+B_C\) further distinguishes within-district allocation from
between-district weighting.

The empirical comparison produces a domain-specific contrast. Cabinet
configurations satisfying the inversion criterion appear in the mandates
generated by all three elections: two after 2014, two after 2018, and one after
2022. Under exact connectedness (\(k=0\)), the ideologically constrained
potential-coalition analysis finds eight inversions in 2014 and six in 2022,
but none in 2018. The nested one-gap domain (\(k=1\)) preserves the 2014 and
2022 inversion regions but also produces a 2018 inversion. Thus, the sharp 2018
contrast between PSC-inclusive cabinet inversions and ideological seat
majorities applies to the exact-connected baseline, not to the one-gap
sensitivity domain. This comparison shows that coalition definition and
configuration determine whether fixed party-level differentials become
majority-changing.

\section{Brazil's multidistrict open-list proportional system}
\label{sec:brazil-system}

\subsection{District architecture and electoral weight}

Brazil does not allocate Chamber seats through a national party-list
calculation. In each of the elections analyzed, the 513 seats were allocated
independently in the twenty-six states and the Federal District. The Federal
District constituted one of these twenty-seven electoral districts and elected
eight deputies; it did not supply adjustment or compensatory seats. Once the
district allocations were completed, the resulting party seats were summed to
form the national Chamber \parencite{camaraDeputadosNumeroEstados}.

District magnitude varied from eight to seventy seats. Eleven of the
twenty-seven districts elected eight deputies, while São Paulo alone elected
seventy. The mean magnitude was nineteen, but the median was ten and the
mode eight. The median and mode are more informative than the mean for the
reviewer's question about a typical district: most Brazilian districts are much
closer to the constitutional minimum than to the maximum. Because there is
no national compensation, rounding losses, effective thresholds, and list
eligibility decisions made in one district are not corrected by party votes cast
elsewhere.

Seats are statutorily distributed according to state population, subject to a
minimum of eight and a maximum of seventy deputies per federative unit.
These constraints create a source of population-to-seat malapportionment
\parencite{samuelsSnyder2001value}. Three distinct quantities should not be
conflated. District magnitude \(M_d\) is the number of seats allocated in
district \(d\), and shapes rounding and the district's implicit threshold.
Legal malapportionment concerns the relation between population share and
seat share. Election-specific electoral weight concerns the relation between
valid-vote share and seat share, and therefore additionally reflects turnout
and the incidence of blank, null, and otherwise nonvalid votes. The
between-district component \(B_C\) introduced below captures the latter
vote-based weighting, not malapportionment alone.

\subsection{Votes, quotas, and remainder seats}

Within each district, federal deputies are elected by open-list proportional
representation. Voters may cast a nominal vote for a candidate or a
party-label vote. Both types of valid vote are credited to the relevant
electoral allocation unit before seats are assigned. Let \(M_d\) denote the
district magnitude, \(V_d\) the valid district vote, and
\(\mathcal{L}_d\) the set of allocation units competing in district \(d\).
Depending on the election, an element \(\ell\in\mathcal{L}_d\) was a party,
a proportional electoral coalition, or a party federation.

The electoral quotient is
\[
QE_d=\rho\left(\frac{V_d}{M_d}\right),
\]
where the statutory rounding operator \(\rho\) discards a fractional part
equal to or below one half and rounds upward when the fraction exceeds one
half. If \(v_{\ell d}\) is the vote of allocation unit \(\ell\), its initial
party quotient is
\[
b_{\ell d}
=
\left\lfloor
\frac{v_{\ell d}}{QE_d}
\right\rfloor .
\]
These quotients assign the initial seats, subject to the applicable
candidate-level eligibility requirement.

Seats left unfilled after this stage are distributed iteratively by highest
averages. If \(s_{\ell d}\) seats have already been assigned to unit \(\ell\),
its next average is
\[
m_{\ell d}(s_{\ell d})
=
\frac{v_{\ell d}}{s_{\ell d}+1}.
\]
The next seat goes to the eligible unit with the highest average, after which
the denominator is updated and the procedure repeated
\parencite[arts.~106--109]{codigoEleitoral1965}.

The remainder sequence uses the divisors \(1,2,3,\ldots\) associated with
Jefferson--D'Hondt. The complete Brazilian procedure should not, however,
be described simply as textbook D'Hondt. It begins with rounded electoral
and party quotients, and it combines this quota-first stage with
election-specific list and candidate eligibility rules. Calling the entire
procedure ``D'Hondt'' would erase precisely the institutional changes that
matter for the comparison across 2014, 2018, and 2022.

\subsection{Proportional electoral coalitions and list apparentement}

The proportional electoral coalitions used in 2014 and 2018 shared the
vote-pooling feature associated with list apparentement, but the two
institutions are not exact synonyms. In a canonical apparentement, separate
lists pool their votes for a first allocation and the alliance's seats are then
distributed among the member lists through a second apportionment
\parencite{pukelsheimLeutgaeb2013apparentements}. A Brazilian
\emph{coligação proporcional} instead presented a single joint open list.
Votes for all member parties and candidates entered the coalition total, and
the coalition's winning candidates were selected by nominal-vote rank across
the entire joint list \parencite{nicolau2006openlist}.

The distinction matters for party-level accounting. Seats attributed ex post
to a member party in 2014 or 2018 were not generated by an independent
second-stage proportional allocation among the coalition partners. They
depended on which candidates from the joint list occupied the winning
positions. The former Brazilian arrangement is therefore a close
institutional relative of apparentement, not an exact implementation of its
canonical two-level formula. The ``lottery'' results associated with
apparentement motivate attention to vote pooling, but should not be imported
as a theorem describing the Brazilian procedure.

These pre-electoral allocation coalitions must also be distinguished from
the post-electoral coalitions analyzed in this article. An observed cabinet
coalition is a set of parties represented in government during a cabinet
period. An ideological coalition is a party set constructed after the election
from the fixed election-year ideological order. Neither is assumed to have
been a joint electoral list.

\subsection{Federations and the 2022 allocation unit}

Proportional electoral coalitions were prohibited before the 2022 general
election. Party federations, introduced in 2021, supplied a more durable
form of interparty grouping. A federation has national scope, must remain
in force for at least four years, and is legally treated as a single party in
electoral and parliamentary activity
\parencite{tse2022federacoes}. Three federations competed in 2022:
Federação Brasil da Esperança, comprising PT, PCdoB, and PV; Federação
PSDB Cidadania; and Federação PSOL REDE.

For seat allocation, a federation pooled the votes of its member parties and
presented a joint ranking of candidates. It therefore constituted the primary
electoral allocation unit. Member parties nevertheless remain substantively
observable in cabinet appointments, party ideology data, and subsequent
political analysis. The baseline results retain member-party labels for that
reason, while the 2022 federation-closure check recalculates every relevant
coalition after adding all members of any federation already intersected by
the coalition. This separates the substantive party-level object from the
institutional constraint imposed by the electoral unit.

\subsection{Fragmentation and sources of disproportionality}

The distinction between party labels and allocation units is especially
important in a fragmented system. In the audited national party-label data,
32 parties received valid votes in 2014, 35 in 2018, and 32 in 2022; 28, 30,
and 23 of those labels, respectively, received at least one seat. Using the
Laakso--Taagepera measure
\[
N=\frac{1}{\sum_i p_i^2},
\]
the effective numbers of electoral parties were
\texttt{[ENEP2014]}, \texttt{[ENEP2018]}, and
\texttt{[ENEP2022]}, while the corresponding effective numbers of
parliamentary parties were
\texttt{[ENPP2014]}, \texttt{[ENPP2018]}, and
\texttt{[ENPP2022]}
\parencite{laakso1979effective}.
These values are generated from the final frozen party table.

The raw party-label count is not the same as the number of electoral lists
entering the formula. In 2014 and 2018, proportional electoral coalitions
were constructed at the district level, so there is no single national count
of primary allocation units. In 2022, the three federations combined seven
party labels into three national allocation identities, reducing the 32 party
labels to 28 party-or-federation identities before accounting for
district-specific candidacy.

This institutional description locates the Brazilian case within Bochsler's
taxonomy. The highest-averages remainder stage supplies a potential
formula effect. District magnitudes generate different natural thresholds and
rounding environments. Legal and candidate-level eligibility rules determine
which votes can obtain particular seats. The eight-to-seventy magnitude
range creates substantial district heterogeneity. Population-based
apportionment subject to floors and ceilings, together with turnout and
valid-vote variation, changes the national electoral weight of district votes.
Electoral geography and the district-specific number of competing lists
determine which parties benefit from those conditions. Finally, coligações
and federations alter the units over which votes are pooled.

The presence of these sources does not establish that each one caused an
observed inversion. It explains why Brazil should not be treated as a
pure-D'Hondt or pure-rounding case. The empirical question is instead how
the resulting party-level differentials, whatever their institutional mixture,
combine across cabinet and ideologically constrained party sets.

These electoral institutions operate inside a presidential system in which
governments ordinarily require multiparty legislative support. The
coalitional-presidentialism literature explains why parties, ministries, and
legislative coordination are the relevant post-electoral objects
\parencite{abranches1988presidencialismo,
figueiredo2000presidential,pereira2004cost}. The analysis does not infer
voter preferences over governments from party votes or claim that every
cabinet party supplies votes uniformly in Congress. It asks the narrower
representational question of whether the party set represented in cabinet,
or admitted by the ideological coalition domain, possesses a Chamber
majority without possessing a corresponding majority of valid
federal-deputy votes.

The raw party-label count is not the same as the number of electoral lists
entering the formula. In 2014 and 2018, proportional electoral coalitions
were constructed at the district level, so there is no single national count
of primary allocation units. In 2022, the three federations combined seven
party labels into three national allocation identities, reducing the 32 party
labels to 28 party-or-federation identities before accounting for
district-specific candidacy.

This institutional description locates the Brazilian case within Bochsler's
taxonomy. The highest-averages remainder stage supplies a potential
formula effect. District magnitudes generate different natural thresholds and
rounding environments. Legal and candidate-level eligibility rules determine
which votes can obtain particular seats. The eight-to-seventy magnitude
range creates substantial district heterogeneity. Population-based
apportionment subject to floors and ceilings, together with turnout and
valid-vote variation, changes the national electoral weight of district votes.
Electoral geography and the district-specific number of competing lists
determine which parties benefit from those conditions. Finally, coligações
and federations alter the units over which votes are pooled.

The presence of these sources does not establish that each one caused an
observed inversion. It explains why Brazil should not be treated as a
pure-D'Hondt or pure-rounding case. The empirical question is instead how
the resulting party-level differentials, whatever their institutional mixture,
combine across cabinet and ideologically constrained party sets.

These electoral institutions operate inside a presidential system in which
governments ordinarily require multiparty legislative support. The
coalitional-presidentialism literature explains why parties, ministries, and
legislative coordination are the relevant post-electoral objects
\parencite{abranches1988presidencialismo,
figueiredo2000presidential,pereira2004cost}. The analysis does not infer
voter preferences over governments from party votes or claim that every
cabinet party supplies votes uniformly in Congress. It asks the narrower
representational question of whether the party set represented in cabinet,
or admitted by the ideological coalition domain, possesses a Chamber
majority without possessing a corresponding majority of valid
federal-deputy votes.
\section{Measurement}
\label{sec:mea}

Following \textcite{miller14_elect_inver_under_propor_repres}, let
\(\Parties\) be the set of parties in a federal-deputy election. For party
\(i\in\Parties\), let \(v_i\) denote its national federal-deputy vote total and
\(s_i\) its Chamber seats. Let \(V=\sum_{i\in\Parties}v_i\) and \(S=513\).
The party's proportional seat quota and seat differential are
\[
q_i=S\frac{v_i}{V},
\qquad
d_i=s_i-q_i.
\]
Positive \(d_i\) indicates more seats than the national vote share implies
under exact proportionality, while negative \(d_i\) indicates fewer. Because
party quotas and seats both sum to \(S\), the party differentials sum to zero.

To compare representation across parties of different sizes, I use the
Taagepera-style representation ratio
\parencite{taagepera1980proportionality}
\[
R_i=\frac{s_i/S}{v_i/V}.
\]
Here \(R_i=1\) denotes exact proportionality, \(R_i>1\) overrepresentation,
and \(R_i<1\) underrepresentation. Parties that receive votes but win no seats
have \(R_i=0\). This ratio supports the party representation profile in
Section~\ref{sec:party}; the absolute differential \(d_i\) is the quantity
aggregated across coalition members.

For any coalition \(C\subseteq\Parties\), define
\[
v_C=\sum_{i\in C}v_i,
\qquad
s_C=\sum_{i\in C}s_i,
\]
and
\[
q_C=S\frac{v_C}{V},
\qquad
d_C=s_C-q_C.
\]
The coalition representation ratio is
\[
R_C=\frac{s_C/S}{v_C/V}
=\frac{s_C}{q_C}
=1+\frac{d_C}{q_C}.
\]
As at the party level, \(R_C=1\) denotes exact proportionality, \(R_C>1\)
coalition overrepresentation, and \(R_C<1\) coalition underrepresentation.
This identity connects the relative and absolute measures. \(R_C-1\) is the
coalition's seat surplus as a proportion of its quota, while \(d_C\) is the
same surplus measured in seats. Equivalently,
\(R_C=\sum_{i\in C}(q_i/q_C)R_i\): the coalition ratio is a quota-weighted mean
of the member parties' representation ratios, so larger parties receive
proportionally greater weight in the coalition measure.

A coalition inversion occurs when a party set has less than half of the
national federal-deputy vote but at least 257 Chamber seats:
\[
\frac{v_C}{V}<.5
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
s_C\geq257.
\]
For a vote-minority coalition, the seat condition is equivalently
\(d_C\geq257-q_C\). This makes clear why \(R_C>1\) is not enough:
overrepresentation records a positive seat surplus, but an inversion occurs
only when that surplus closes the coalition's remaining distance to 257.

Because \(C\) and its complement \(\bar C\) partition the election-year
party set, they also partition the valid federal-deputy vote and all 513
Chamber seats. Hence
\[
\frac{v_C}{V}<\frac{1}{2},\qquad s_C\geq257
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
\frac{v_{\bar C}}{V}>\frac{1}{2},\qquad s_{\bar C}\leq256.
\]
Thus an inversion implies \(v_C<v_{\bar C}\) while
\(s_C>s_{\bar C}\): unlike a manufactured majority at the party level,
the coalition-versus-complement comparison necessarily contains a reversal.

Throughout the article, \(V\) denotes all valid federal-deputy votes. I do
not reclassify cases using a denominator restricted to seat-winning parties,
threshold-surviving electoral units, or another effective-vote subset.


To locate the coalition differential in Brazil's multidistrict aggregation, let
\(v_{Cd}\) and \(s_{Cd}\) be the coalition's votes and attributed seats in
district \(d\), and let \(V_d\) and \(S_d\) be the corresponding district
totals. The within-district allocation component is
\[
A_C=
\sum_d
\left(
s_{Cd}
-
S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
\right),
\]
and the between-district weighting component is
\[
B_C=
\sum_d
S_d\frac{v_{Cd}}{V_d}
-
S\frac{v_C}{V}.
\]
Together they reproduce the national coalition differential:
\[
d_C=A_C+B_C.
\]
The first term compares attributed seats with proportional quotas inside
districts. The second compares the sum of district quota contributions with the
coalition's single national quota.

This is an accounting identity, not a causal decomposition. In particular,
\(B_C\) is not pure malapportionment: it jointly reflects district seat
weights, turnout and valid-vote differences, and coalition vote geography.
Appendix~\ref{app:accounting-diagnostics} contains the focal
party-contribution table and the district electoral-weight diagnostic. Exact
implementation checks and richer machine-readable accounting diagnostics
remain available in the replication outputs.

The two components provide a coalition-specific partition that is narrower
than a complete decomposition of unequal representation. In relation to
\textcite{bochsler2026winners}, \(A_C\) absorbs the net consequences of
within-district allocation, including quota and remainder rounding,
district-specific effective thresholds, candidate eligibility, list pooling,
and the number and distribution of competing allocation units. District
magnitude affects this component by changing both discretization and the
difficulty of obtaining representation.

The component \(B_C\) instead records the difference between weighting
district coalition shares by district seats and weighting individual votes
equally in the national total. It therefore combines population-to-seat
apportionment, turnout and valid-vote variation, and coalition geography.
District magnitude also enters this term through the district seat weights.
Because several institutional sources enter each component, there is no
one-to-one mapping from \(A_C\) or \(B_C\) to a single electoral mechanism.
The identity locates the coalition differential in the national accounting; it
does not estimate the causal effect of changing any one rule.
\section{Data}
\label{sec:data}

Vote and seat data for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 elections come from the Tribunal Superior
Eleitoral (TSE), accessed through the \texttt{electionsBR} package
\parencite{electionsbr}.\footnote{The 2022 seat vector is based on the official
electoral data available when the dataset used in this analysis was constructed.
Subsequent judicial decisions produced additional retotalizations of the 2022
election and altered the allocation of a small number of seats across parties.
These later revisions are not incorporated here. The analysis therefore refers
to a fixed vintage of the official electoral data rather than to an ex post
reconstruction incorporating subsequent litigation.} Cabinet composition is reconstructed from the
Brazilian Wikipedia cabinet-member lists for the Dilma Rousseff, Michel Temer,
Jair Bolsonaro, and Lula III administrations. These sources identify ministerial
appointments by office, incumbent, party label, entry date, and exit date. I use
these appointment records to construct cabinet periods, defined by changes in
the set of parties holding ministries or ministry-level offices\footnote{Further
  implementation details, including party-label harmonization and translation
  tables, are documented in the replication repository: \url{https://anonymous.4open.science/r/electoral-inversion-B6B5/README.md}.}.

Cabinet periods are linked to elections by mandate-window overlap. The 2014
election is linked to cabinet periods active from January 1, 2015 through
December 31, 2018; the 2018 election to periods active from January 1, 2019
through December 31, 2022; and the 2022 election to the observed Lula III window
beginning on January 1, 2023 and continuing through March 19, 2026, the latest reliable cabinet-period endpoint in the source data. Coalition vote and seat totals are computed using the party labels and
party-level totals from the election that produced the corresponding Chamber
delegation. These inputs are used to construct two analytical objects:
observed cabinet-period coalitions and ideologically constrained potential
coalitions defined over the fixed election-year party order.
Each party set is evaluated by its national vote share, seat share, quota, seat
differential, and coalition-inversion status.

The national vote measure is the valid federal-deputy vote credited to each
party under its election-year label. This includes both nominal votes and
party-label votes, including valid nominal votes later treated as party-label
votes in the TSE aggregation.\footnote{For 2014, the vote measure is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS + QT\_VOTOS\_LEGENDA}. For 2018 and 2022, it is
\texttt{QT\_VOTOS\_NOMINAIS\_VALIDOS + QT\_TOTAL\_VOTOS\_LEG\_VALIDOS}.}

The primary electoral allocation unit and the substantive party unit are not
identical in every election. In 2014 and 2018, votes were first pooled at the
party or proportional-coalition level within each district. Seats attributed
to coalition-member parties are therefore ex post party accounting
attributions based on the elected candidates emerging from the joint open
list. In 2022, votes were first pooled at the party or federation level.
Party-level votes and seats are retained because cabinet participation and
ideological positions are observed by member party, but federation closure is
reported as a sensitivity analysis.

For a 2022 party set \(C\), federation closure adds every member of a
federation whenever \(C\) contains at least one of its member parties. Votes,
seats, quotas, differentials, and inversion status are then recalculated for
the closed set. This check is applied to the observed 2023 cabinet inversion
and to the 2022 ideological-coalition results. No analogous closure is imposed
for the temporary and district-specific proportional electoral coalitions of
2014 and 2018, because those groupings did not constitute durable
post-electoral organizations.

The ideology analysis uses external party classifications to order parties on a
left-right scale. The 2014 and 2018 orders use \textcite{bolognesi2023nova}. The 2022 order
uses the later 2018--2022 update, operationalized through the 2025 data release
and associated article \parencite{bolognesi2025dataverse,bolognesi2026desaparecimento}.
This creates a methodological caveat: the 2014 ordering retrojects later expert
classifications onto an earlier party system. The complete election-year
ideology orders, classifications, source labels, and translation details are
documented in the replication materials.

\section{Cabinet coalitional inversions}
\label{sec:cabinets}

Cabinet composition provides the first coalition domain. The relevant object is
the set of parties actually represented in cabinet during each period, rather
than a broader legislative support bloc or an election-level governing
alternative. Changes in ministerial party participation generate successive
realized party sets within a mandate while the underlying election results
remain fixed.

Only a small number of cabinet transitions are needed to locate the inversion
periods.\footnote{Appendix~\ref{app:composition} reports every cabinet period,
  its translated election-year party composition, and entries and exits relative

  post-fusion UNIÃO into DEM and PSL for 2018 vote--seat accounting.}
During the 2014-linked mandate, the two-day 2016.2 inversion appears after PRB
and PP have left the cabinet party set but before PSD leaves. The later 2017.1

configuration reaches exactly 257 seats; the same translated election-space

2022, ending the inversion. The 2022-linked mandate begins with the 2023.1
inversion; the entry of PP and Republicanos in 2023.2 expands the coalition

after 2018, and the initial cabinet formation after 2022.


\section{Ideologically constrained potential coalitions}
\label{sec:ideology}
The second coalition domain complements observed cabinet composition with a
systematic set of potential party combinations. Rather than searching all
subsets of parties, I restrict the baseline domain using Axelrod's connectedness
criterion: eligible coalitions must form contiguous sets in the election-year
ideological ordering. This does not imply that actual Brazilian cabinets are
normally connected, nor is connectedness treated as a theory of coalition
formation. It provides a transparent restriction on the otherwise combinatorial
space of possible party sets.

Formally, let \(p_1,\ldots,p_n\) denote the fixed election-year ideological
order. For any nonempty coalition \(C\), let \(\operatorname{span}(C)\) contain
every party from its leftmost to its rightmost member, and define
\[
g(C)=\lvert \operatorname{span}(C)\setminus C\rvert,
\qquad
\mathcal D_k=\{C:g(C)\leq k\}.
\]
Exact connectedness is retained as the baseline, \(k=0\), corresponding to
Axelrod's contiguous connected-coalition restriction
\parencite{axelrod1970conflict}. I then consider the nested one-gap domain,
\(k=1\), in which a coalition may omit at most one party located between its
ideological endpoints. Thus, every member of \(\mathcal D_1\) is either a full
interval or a full interval with one interior party removed. This relaxation
preserves ideological proximity as the restriction on the coalition space while
preventing a single intermediate party from determining admissibility. Because
the underlying estimated party order is held fixed, the exercise tests
sensitivity to exact no-gap connectedness rather than to ideology measurement
generally.

Minimality is defined relative to each admissible domain. A coalition \(C\) is
a minimal seat-majority coalition in \(\mathcal D_k\) if
\[
s_C\geq257
\quad\text{and there is no}\quad
C'\subsetneq C
\quad\text{such that}\quad
C'\in\mathcal D_k\ \text{and}\ s_{C'}\geq257.
\]
For \(k=0\), this reproduces the minimal connected winning coalitions of
\textcite{axelrod1970conflict}; in that special case, testing the two endpoint
deletions is sufficient. For \(k=1\), interior deletions can remain admissible,
so the computation instead tests exact set inclusion over all coalitions in the
domain.

The realized and potential-coalition analyses therefore answer different
questions. Cabinet
composition identifies party sets that were institutionally realized, while the
ideological domain identifies structured coalition possibilities that need not
have entered government. Comparing them shows whether inversion is confined to
particular realized configurations or also appears in ideologically restricted
regions of the party system.

become seat majorities only after also crossing the vote-majority threshold,
even though two PSC-inclusive cabinet configurations based on the same election
invert. It does not describe the \(k=1\) result.

By 2022, the inversion region has moved to the right edge of the party order.
MDB--UNIÃO spans a relatively broad center-right-to-far-right interval, whereas
PP--PL is already a narrow right-side majority without a vote majority.
Exact-connected minimal winning intervals that include PT remain broad
vote-and-seat majorities rather than inversions.
Appendix~\ref{app:cabinet-interval-bridge}
documents this distinction in detail: observed cabinets are sparse selections
from wider ideological spans, not simply the connected intervals plotted here.


\section{Comparing decomposition patterns across coalition domains}
\label{sec:decomposition}

Tables~\ref{tab:cabinet-inversions} and \ref{tab:minimal-intervals} make
coalition differentials directly comparable across realized cabinets and
ideologically constrained potential coalitions. Because \(d_C=q_C(R_C-1)\),
\(d_C\) is the absolute seat surplus behind the representation ratio. The
diagnostics then proceed in two accounting steps: member-party \(d_i\) entries
show which parties add to or offset that national surplus, and \(A_C\) and
\(B_C\) divide the same total between within-district allocation and
between-district weighting.

Party-level diagnostics reveal a further distinction across configurations.
Every focal inversion combines positive and negative member-party
differentials, so inversion does not require every coalition member to be
overrepresented. No single party accounts for a majority of the gross positive
contribution in any focal case, although one or two parties often lead the
positive side. In PP--PL, the combined accounting contributions of PL and PP
equal \AcctPPPLPLPPSharePct\% of the coalition's net \(d_C\), while UNIÃO is
the second-largest individual positive contributor. The shared 2018 cabinet
vector is more mixed: its positive total is distributed across five parties,
and the coalition still inverts with PSL at
\(d_i=\AcctCabinetTwentyEighteenPSLDifferential\). These are ex post
accounting patterns, not party-specific causal effects. Appendix~
\ref{app:party-contributions} reports the complete focal-case diagnostics.

Three patterns appear. First, the components reinforce one another when
\(A_C>0\) and \(B_C>0\). This occurs in cabinet period 2016.2, the 2014
PTB--PR interval, and the 2022 PP--PL interval. Second, a positive
within-district component is partly
offset by negative between-district weighting in 2017.1, both 2018 cabinet
periods, 2023.1, and three of the four 2014 ideological inversions. The shared
2018 cabinet vector has \(A_C=19.07\) and \(B_C=-4.45\), illustrating how a
substantial positive within-district term can coexist with a smaller national
differential.

Third, MDB--UNIÃO has the qualitatively different pattern
\(A_C<0,B_C>0\): its \(-1.29\)-seat within-district component is more than
offset by a \(9.88\)-seat between-district component. This is the clearest case
of between-district dominance, while PP--PL is the clearest 2022 case of
reinforcement. These are comparative accounting patterns, not causal
mechanisms. In particular, \(B_C\) jointly reflects district seat weights,
turnout and valid-vote differences, and coalition vote geography.


\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:disc}

The analysis compares coalition inversions across two politically defined
domains in a proportional system institutionally different from the principal
cases in the existing literature. The first domain follows the parties actually
represented in cabinet as composition changes during a mandate. The second
enumerates potential coalitions restricted by ideological proximity, first
under exact connectedness and then under a nested one-gap relaxation. The same
party vote--seat distribution can therefore be evaluated under realized and
structured potential recombinations.

The distinction matters because coalition inversion is
configuration-dependent. Under exact connectedness, the 2018-linked mandate
provides the sharpest within-election contrast: two PSC-inclusive cabinet
periods reach exactly 257 seats without a vote majority, while no ideological
interval inverts. That null is sensitive to the no-gap restriction, however:
the one-gap domain contains a PCdoB--PODE inversion omitting PSDB, with 47.82
percent of the vote and 257 seats. By contrast, the substantive locations of
the strongest 2014 and 2022 ideological inversions are robust. Their one-gap
counterparts retain the PTB--PR and PP--PL spans, respectively, while omitting
one intermediate party and becoming slightly stronger. Observed cabinets and
ideologically constrained potential coalitions are therefore complementary
objects, not interchangeable definitions of the same coalition domain.

The party representation profiles also show why coalition inversion should not
be reduced to one stable party-size bias. In 2014, overrepresentation and
underrepresentation are distributed across parties of different sizes. By 2022,
larger parties are more consistently overrepresented, but coalition outcomes
still depend on which positive and negative party differentials are combined.
The political consequence of a fixed electoral result is realized through the
party sets that organize legislative and governing majorities.

At the coalition level, \(R_C\) provides the bridge between party
representation and majority status. It is a quota-weighted aggregation of the
member-party ratios, not a simple count of overrepresented parties. The cabinet
timeline shows that relative advantage is common: 21 of 24 party sets have
\(R_C>1\), but only five invert. Majority-changing advantage depends jointly
on the size of \(R_C-1\) and on \(q_C\), because the latter fixes how many
surplus seats are needed to reach 257. The contrast between the
threshold-adjacent MDB--UNIÃO interval and the more strongly overrepresented
PP--PL interval makes this distinction especially clear.

The decomposition of the exact-connected baseline adds a second comparative
result. The within-district component is positive in all five observed cabinet
inversions and in five of the six endpoint-minimal ideological inversions.
Between-district weighting does
not have a uniform role: it reinforces \(A_C\) in 2016.2, PTB--PR, and PP--PL,
but offsets a positive \(A_C\) in the other cabinet configurations and three
2014 ideological cases. MDB--UNIÃO is the important exception in the other
direction, with a negative within-district component more than offset by a
positive between-district component. The balance between the two terms thus
differs across coalition configurations even when every row satisfies the same
inversion criterion.

These comparisons locate national differentials in the accounting without
assigning causal responsibility. \(B_C\) is not pure malapportionment; it
combines district seat weights, turnout and valid-vote differences, and
coalition vote geography. Likewise, party contributions in 2014 and 2018 are ex
post accounting attributions in elections where seats were often allocated
through joint electoral lists. A large positive or negative party or district
entry does not establish that the unit caused an inversion.

Brazil's multiple sources of disproportionality do not undermine the
aggregation argument, but they delimit its interpretation. The analysis does
not show that district magnitude, malapportionment, turnout, electoral
coalitions, federations, thresholds, or rounding individually produced an
inversion. It shows that the realized party differentials generated by this
institutional environment can accumulate across particular political sets and
cross a discontinuous majority threshold. In a pure-PR benchmark the
whole-number problem is sufficient for this possibility; in Brazil it is one
potential source among several.

Institutional complexity therefore weakens a monocausal claim but strengthens
the case for studying the coalition transformation itself. Party differentials
that appear modest or politically inconsequential in isolation may reinforce
one another inside one coalition, cancel inside another, and leave a third just
below 257 seats. The relevant contribution is not a claim that Brazil reveals
a new source of disproportionality. It is the demonstration that ordinary
differentials from several sources acquire a distinct institutional consequence
when parties are recombined into realized or constrained potential majorities.

This limited claim nevertheless places the Brazilian cases within the broader
literature on paradoxes and tradeoffs under proportional representation. Party-level
proportionality does not settle the majority status of every post-electoral
party set. Coalition inversions identify the narrower circumstance in which the
ordinary vote--seat translation, once aggregated across a politically relevant
set, yields a legislative majority without a corresponding national vote
majority \parencite{van1993paradoxes,kurrild2008voting,
kaminski18_spoil_effec_propor_repres_system,
FlisGrofmanKaminski2025Introduction}.

Two further qualifications remain. The one-gap check weakens exact
connectedness while holding the same estimated party order fixed. It therefore
addresses whether one intermediate rank mechanically determines admissibility,
but it does not incorporate uncertainty in the ideology estimates themselves;
alternative placements may change endpoints, spans, or marginal cases. In
addition, the vote share of a cabinet or potential coalition is the sum of votes
cast for parties later grouped into that set, not a direct vote for the
coalition. The criterion measures coalitional representation; it does not
measure voter endorsement, legislative discipline, roll-call support, or
portfolio bargaining.


\section{Conclusion}

This article extends the literature on coalition inversions by following
cabinet composition across legislative terms, comparing those realized party
sets with ideologically constrained potential coalitions, and examining both in
a multidistrict proportional system without national compensation. Party-level
deviations can aggregate into politically consequential coalition
differentials, but whether they change majority status depends on coalition
configuration.

The empirical comparison is not uniform across elections or domains. Five
observed cabinet periods invert: two linked to the 2014 election, two
PSC-inclusive periods linked to 2018, and the initial Lula cabinet linked to
2022. Under exact connectedness, ideological inversions appear in 2014 and
2022 but not in 2018, and PP--PL provides a compact right-side inversion in
2022. Allowing one omitted intermediate party preserves the strongest 2014 and
2022 inversion spans, with slightly larger vote deficits, but reveals that the
2018 ideological null is sensitive to the exact no-gap restriction.

The coalition representation ratio clarifies the threshold logic. Most observed
cabinet coalitions are overrepresented, but only five combine that advantage
with a vote minority and a seat majority. \(R_C\) measures relative advantage,
\(d_C\) measures its absolute seat value, and \(q_C\) determines the
coalition's proportional distance from 257. Coalition inversion is therefore
not synonymous with overrepresentation. It is the subset of overrepresented
configurations in which the accumulated seat surplus is majority-changing.

Brazil's multiple sources of disproportionality do not undermine the
aggregation argument, but they delimit its interpretation. The analysis does
not show that district magnitude, malapportionment, turnout, electoral
coalitions, federations, thresholds, or rounding individually produced an
inversion. It shows that the realized party differentials generated by this
institutional environment can accumulate across particular political sets and
cross a discontinuous majority threshold. In a pure-PR benchmark the
whole-number problem is sufficient for this possibility; in Brazil it is one
potential source among several.

Institutional complexity therefore weakens a monocausal claim but strengthens
the case for studying the coalition transformation itself. Party differentials
that appear modest or politically inconsequential in isolation may reinforce
one another inside one coalition, cancel inside another, and leave a third just
below 257 seats. The relevant contribution is not a claim that Brazil reveals
a new source of disproportionality. It is the demonstration that ordinary
differentials from several sources acquire a distinct institutional consequence
when parties are recombined into realized or constrained potential majorities.

\section{Cabinet coalitions and ideological intervals}
\label{app:cabinet-interval-bridge}

All references to ideological intervals, connected closures, and minimal
connected coalitions in this appendix section concern the exact-connected
\(k=0\) baseline. The one-gap result is summarized separately in
Table~\ref{tab:interval-summary}.

Table \ref{tab:cabinet-interval-bridge} compares observed cabinet coalitions
with the ideological intervals that contain or approximate them. The bridge
table keeps two operations separate: cabinet periods are defined from the party
labels observed in the cabinet data, while ideological closures and vote-seat
calculations use the translated election-year party set. The interval analysis
therefore remains tied to the Chamber delegation produced by each election.
The connected closure of a cabinet is the smallest no-gap ideological interval
containing all cabinet parties. Gaps are parties inside that closure that are
not members of the cabinet coalition. The final columns report the closest
minimal connected winning interval and, where one exists, the closest minimal
connected inversion. This table links the observed coalition analysis to the
interval analysis by showing whether realized cabinet coalitions are compact
ideological blocs or sparse selections from a wider ideological span.

\input{table_appendix_cabinet_interval_bridge.tex}

The first result is that no observed cabinet coalition is itself a contiguous
ideological interval. Every cabinet period has gaps inside its connected closure.
The smallest number of gaps is eight, and several cabinet periods have more than
fifteen. This means that Brazilian cabinet coalitions are not simply connected
ideological blocs translated into government. They are selective coalitions: party
sets assembled from a wider ideological span while omitting many parties that
lie between their leftmost and rightmost members.

This matters for the interpretation of cabinet inversions. The observed cabinet
inversions are sparse coalitions, but their connected closures are not inversions.
All connected closures in Table \ref{tab:cabinet-interval-bridge} are
vote-and-seat majorities. The 2016.2 cabinet, for example, has 46.54 percent of
the vote and 259 seats, but its connected closure runs from PCdoB to PR and
contains 20 parties. That closure has 77.61 percent of the vote and 408 seats.
The inversion therefore does not come from a compact left-to-right ideological
bloc. It comes from a selective cabinet coalition that retains a seat majority
while omitting thirteen parties inside its ideological span.

The same logic applies to the more durable Temer-period inversion. The 2017.1
cabinet contains DEM, PMDB, PP, PPS, PSB, PSD, PSDB, and PV. Its connected
closure runs from PSB to DEM, contains 25 parties, and has seventeen gaps. The
observed cabinet has 49.91 percent of the vote and 266 seats, while the full
PSB--DEM closure has 78.42 percent of the vote and 409 seats. The cabinet
inversion is therefore not a connected ideological majority. It is a sparse
center-to-right cabinet coalition located near the connected inversion region
identified in the interval analysis. Its closest minimal connected winning
interval is PSB--PTN, which is itself an inversion with 48.92 percent of the vote
and 260 seats.

The Dilma coalitions have a different ideological profile from the later
Temer coalitions. They are also sparse, but they are left-anchored. The cabinet
sets before Temer include PT, PCdoB, and PDT while extending to parties such
as PMDB, PSD, PR, PP, and PTB. Their closures run from PCdoB to either PR or
PP, and their gap structure contains many center, center-right, and right parties
that are not cabinet members. The impeachment transition changes this structure.
From 2016.3 to 2016.4, PCdoB, PDT, PT, and PR leave, while PSB, PPS, PV, PSD,
PSDB, PP, and DEM enter. In the ideology classification, the parties leaving are
concentrated on the left and center-left, with one right party, while the parties
entering are concentrated in the center, right, and far right. The cabinet remains
punctured, but its ideological anchor shifts rightward.

The ideology diagnostics in the table confirm this transition. On the project's
ideology scale, the average unweighted cabinet ideology rises from the
pre-Temer sequence to the post-impeachment Temer sequence. The transition from
2016.3 to 2016.4 alone increases the unweighted cabinet ideology mean by 1.53
points and the seat-weighted mean by 1.49 points. This is the substantive
difference between the two sparse coalition types. The pre-Temer coalitions are
left-anchored and broad. The Temer coalitions are center-to-right and broad.
Both are non-contiguous, but they occupy different regions of the ideological
order.

The Bolsonaro-period cabinets provide a domain contrast. They are more
ideologically concentrated on the right and far right, and their connected
closures are narrower than the 2014 and 2022 closures. The PSC-inclusive
2021.3 and 2022.1 cabinets each have 47.25 percent of the vote and exactly 257
seats, so both observed coalitions are inversions. Their connected closure,
PSD--DEM, contains seventeen parties and eight gaps, has 57.37 percent of the
vote and 292 seats, and is therefore a vote-and-seat majority. The closest
minimal connected winning interval is PSDB--DEM, which has 51.52 percent of the
vote and 257 seats. Thus, the 2018 mandate contains observed cabinet inversions
but no exact-connected ideological-interval inversion: the sparse realized
party set and its connected closure have different majority classifications.
The one-gap inversion reported in Table~\ref{tab:interval-summary} is a distinct
sensitivity result.


The 2023.1 Lula cabinet returns to a left-anchored structure, but it is not a
compact left coalition. It contains PSOL, PCdoB, PT, PDT, PSB, REDE, MDB,
PSD, and UNIÃO. Its connected closure runs from PSOL to UNIÃO, contains 26
parties, and has seventeen gaps. The observed cabinet is an inversion, with
48.89 percent of the vote and 263 seats, but its connected closure has 80.64
percent of the vote and 407 seats. The closest minimal connected winning
interval is PT--PP, a broad vote-and-seat majority with 57.86 percent of the
vote and 284 seats. The closest minimal connected inversion is MDB--UNIÃO,
which lies to the right of the cabinet's left anchor.

This comparison is crucial for interpreting the 2022 result. The Lula cabinet is
a realized inversion, but it is not the compact connected inversion revealed by
the interval search. The compact connected inversion is PP--PL: an eight-party
right/far-right interval with 45.35 percent of the vote and 258 seats. The
observed Lula coalition reaches a seat majority through a broad and sparse
cross-ideological cabinet arrangement. The exact-connected interval analysis shows
that the most compact vote-minority seat-majority bloc in the same Chamber is
located on the right.

The two analyses therefore meet at a specific structural point. Cabinet
coalitions identify realized governing party sets. Connected intervals identify
the seat-vote geometry of ideological blocs. The cabinet-to-interval comparison
shows that realized cabinets may draw on seat asymmetries without being
connected blocs themselves. In 2014, the pre-Temer coalitions are sparse and
left-anchored, while the Temer coalitions are sparse and shifted rightward. In
2018, two right-side cabinets reach exactly 257 seats without a vote majority,
while their connected closures have both vote and seat majorities. In 2022, the
Lula cabinet is a broad realized inversion, while the compact connected
inversion lies on the right. The gap between cabinet coalitions and connected
intervals is therefore not a nuisance. It shows how Brazil's coalition
presidentialism combines sparse cabinet construction with an ideological
distribution in which seat-efficient connected blocs are often located elsewhere.
